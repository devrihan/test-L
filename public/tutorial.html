Reading Your LOAM Dashboard

LOAM takes your real exam papers — scanned, marked, question by question — and turns them into something more useful than a total: it shows you where the marks went and what to do about it.

Everything on your dashboard comes from your own answer sheets. When you see handwriting, that's your paper; a mark like 1.5/4 is your score on that question out of what it carried.

Privacy, up front: you only ever see your own answers. Class-wide numbers ("your section", "class average") are combined figures — never a classmate's paper or name.

Part 2

How marks work here

Three conventions, worth thirty seconds because they're easy to misread:

"Not marked yet" is not zero. Unmarked work never counts as a zero; averages are calculated from marked papers only.

Marks are per whole question. A long question with parts (i) and (ii) shows one combined score out of the full marks the question carried — 1.5/4 means one-and-a-half of four across both parts.

Colour is the verdict. Wherever you see green/orange/red on a chart: green is full marks, orange is partial, red is zero — and no bar at all means nothing was earned there.

Part 3

Exams: one paper, two tabs

Open Exams and pick a paper. The page has two tabs — Summary and Analysis — and reading them in that order is the point.

The Summary tab: the verdict

Three things, no scrolling: your Score (say 15/35), a headline verdict written from your paper — something like "Build your basics in Solutions first" — and a short plan under it saying where the marks went and where to start. It's the one-minute answer to "how did this exam go?". The View personalised analysis button takes you to the second tab. Below the verdict sits Common mistakes — the traps your section fell into on this exam; where your own answer shows one, your answer appears as the example.

The Analysis tab: where the marks went

Read it right to left: the long questions (Q16–Q20) sit at the end, carry the most marks, and are usually where the orange is. One orange 5-marker outweighs three green 1-markers.

Below the chart, a question browser lets you open each answer without leaving the page — same layout as the Questions page, so everything in Part 5 applies here too. A Score distribution table shows how your marks spread across the paper.

Part 5

Questions: your answer, side by side with the question

Going wrong is a diagnosis of your actual working, not a generic comment — it names the exact step where the marks left. Next practise turns that into the thing to do about it. Read them as a pair: what happened, then so what.

Part 6

Know your mistake types

Every mistake on your dashboard carries one of eight labels. They tell you what kind of fix each one needs — and the dashboard lists them from the kind that takes the most work to fix to the kind that takes the least. Knowing the type is most of the battle.

FoundationsConceptsFormulasReasoningStepsMisreadNotationSlips

On the dashboard, bars like this show how your mistakes split across the types — always in this order, from the deepest fix (a foundation) to the cheapest (a slip), and always in these colours.

Foundational gap (earlier-class skill)

This year's method was fine — a skill from an earlier class gave way underneath it: signs, square roots, solving a simple inequality. These hide inside harder questions, which is why they sting.

What to do: fix the old skill directly, on easy examples, until it's boring. One repaired foundation usually lifts marks in several chapters at once.

Concept mix-up

Two ideas got merged, or a rule was used somewhere it doesn't apply — like cancelling matrices as if they were numbers.

What to do: this is the type to take to your teacher. One conversation that untangles the two ideas is worth ten practice papers.

Formula recall

The plan was right, but a formula or identity came out wrong from memory — a half-angle identity flipped, an inverse formula misremembered. Everything after that step was doomed no matter how carefully you worked.

What to do: keep a one-page formula sheet per chapter and test yourself on it — write the formulas out blank, don't just reread them. Rereading feels like knowing.

Incomplete reasoning

Your final answer may even be right — but the argument didn't cover every case, or asserted something without showing it. In "prove" and "show that" questions, the reasoning is the answer, and marks are given for it.

What to do: before boxing an answer, ask "have I shown this, or just said it?" In case-based proofs, count your cases against the possibilities — the mixed case is the one everyone forgets.

Mistake in the steps

You knew what to do and used the right method from this year's syllabus — the mistake happened while carrying out the steps. That's genuinely good news compared to a concept gap: nothing needs relearning.

What to do: redo the same question slowly and find the step that broke. Then do two more like it. Speed comes back on its own; accuracy has to be rebuilt deliberately.

Misread or incomplete answer

The maths you wrote may even be correct — but it answered a different question than the one asked, or stopped before reaching the thing that was asked for. The examiner can only credit the question on the paper.

What to do: read the question twice before starting, and after finishing ask one question: "did I produce the thing it asked for?" If it says "hence", use the result it just gave you.

Notation & presentation

The mathematics was there, but written in a form that can't be credited — brackets that change the meaning, a missing unit, an answer left in the wrong form.

What to do: check the last line against what the question asked for: right form, right notation, clearly stated. These are quick fixes worth real marks.

The pattern to notice: most mistakes on most papers are steps, foundations and slips. Those don't need you to relearn anything. They need slower, checked working on questions you already know how to do.

Small slip (copying/sign/arithmetic)

One copying error, one sign, one addition — in work that was otherwise right. These are the cheapest marks on the whole paper to win back.

What to do: build one habit: when you copy a line, glance back at the line you copied it from. Most slips happen between lines, not within them.

Part 7

A five-minute routine that works

  1. Open the exam's Summary tab and read the verdict — it names your starting point.
  2. On the chapter you're revising, look at the concept breakdown and find the short bar.
  3. Hover the question chips and try to answer each one in your head, today.
  4. Read Your mistakes in this chapter — note the types, not just the questions. Steps/slips/foundations: redo those exact questions slowly. Concept mix-ups: write down the one question you'd ask your teacher, and ask it.
  5. On the Questions page, re-read Going wrong for anything orange in the question-wise chart, and do what Next practise says.
